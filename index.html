Personal — not for anyone else's eyes

The Kit

For when the pressure builds and you need somewhere for it to go besides out at a person, or nowhere at all.

Not suppression. Not explosion. Feel it, then move it. Name it for a breath, then reach for whatever fits where you are and how much time you've got.
Filter by where you are
In the moment under 2 min · anywhere

Name it, silently

One word, no story. "Angry." "Offended." "Hurt." Let it register in your body for one breath before you do anything else. This alone lowers the charge.

Discharge, in place

Wall push-offs, fast air punches, shake your limbs out like flinging off water. Spend the adrenaline, don't wait to calm down first.

Cold water

Wrists, face. Blunts the physiological spike fast — it's a real reset button, not just a distraction.

Hold the tears at the brim

Breathe slowly through your nose, unfocus your eyes for a second, press your tongue to the roof of your mouth. You're still feeling it — you're just managing the output for the room you're in.

Clench and release

Full-body tension for 10 seconds, then drop it. A few rounds. Works seated, silent, in the dark, next to someone asleep.

Vocal & expressive 5–20 min

Sing it loud and ugly

Not performance-pretty. You already have the instrument — use it as a tool on purpose, not just for joy.

Scream it out

Into a pillow, or in the car with the windows up. Full volume, no audience.

Growl low

Humming or growling in your chest — releases the tension without needing volume. Good for quiet hours or public places.

Dance until you're not composed

No choreography. Move until the charge actually drops, not just until you feel silly for starting.

Ugly journal dump

Ballpoint pen, no punctuation, no rereading. Get it out of your body and onto the page — this isn't for anyone, including future you.

Fast walk

Around the block, around the store. Good for when you need to physically leave a space, not just sit in it.

The bigger containers longer / planned

Working out

The main one. Something to push against — weights especially. Actually metabolizes the cortisol spike, not just distracts from it.

Shadow boxing / hit a pillow

Targeted release, built for anger specifically. Gives the impulse somewhere to land that isn't a person.

Self-pleasure

Full unrestrained release, on your own terms, always available. Not the only valve — but a real one.

Diamond painting or building

The come-down, after the charge is already spent. Occupies your hands, or channels the energy into something architectural — very you.

Weighted pressure

Weighted blanket, or your own arms wrapped tight around yourself. Deep pressure calms the nervous system directly.

Cognitive redirect stuck somewhere, can't move

Count backward by 7s

From 100. Occupies the analytical brain just enough to break the loop without becoming another bypass.

5-4-3-2-1 grounding

Name 5 things you see, 4 you hear, 3 you feel. Useful when the anger tips into overwhelm rather than staying contained.

A different job than release: this is about noticing before it builds — catching yourself when you've checked out, and coming back into your body on purpose.
The anchor phrase

When you notice the physical signs starting — tears rising, heat, the urge to move, the pull to check out — say this, out loud or in your head:

"Something's happening."

Not analyzing it. Just tagging it as it's occurring. It tells your mind: we can pay attention, it's okay, we don't have to check out. It's the bridge between body-knows and mind-knows.

Body scan

A slow pass through your body to notice what's actually there, instead of staying in your head on autopilot.

Start at the top of your head. Just notice — tight, relaxed, warm, nothing in particular.
Move down slowly: jaw, shoulders, chest, stomach, hands, legs, feet.
At each spot, don't fix or change anything. Just notice it's there.
If your mind wanders off or you go back on autopilot, that's normal — just come back to the next spot.

Good for work, for robot-mode moments, for whenever you notice you've retreated into your head and gone on autopilot.

5-4-3-2-1 grounding
5 things you can see
4 things you can hear
3 things you can feel (texture, temperature, weight)
2 things you can smell
1 thing you can taste

Works in low-stimulation moments and high-stimulation ones alike. The hearing part tends to work especially well for pulling you back into the room.

Get Quiet ~90 sec, in order

For when the music in your head won't stop and you want silence. Do these in sequence.

Cold shock (optional, 10 sec) — if you're near water, splash it on your wrists or face.
Breath reset (30 sec) — inhale 4 counts, exhale 8 counts. Four rounds. The long exhale is what actually shifts you.
Ground through senses (30 sec) — name 5 things you see, 4 you hear, 3 you can touch. Let your eyes actually land on each one.
Physical anchor (20 sec) — press your feet flat into the floor and notice the sensation. Or press your palms together hard for 10 seconds, then release.

If the music's still there after — your mind's likely understimulated, not overstimulated. Give it something absorbing instead of trying to force silence: a walk, stretching, dishes with full attention.

Body Check-In training yourself to feel it before it ruptures

Daily — 5 min body scan

Lie down or sit still. Move attention slowly from feet up to head. At each area, just notice — tight, warm, numb, nothing. No fixing, no judging. Just data.

3 check-in points — waking, lunch, before bed

Ask "what am I feeling in my body" before "what am I thinking." Order matters — your mind will answer for the body if you let it go first.

Catching attachment early — things you value

Pause sometimes and ask: what does having this feel like in my body right now? Not why you like it — where you feel it. Ease in the chest. Settledness in the stomach.

Catching attachment early — small irritations

When something mildly bothers you, pause before naming it mentally. Ask: where in my body is this? That's the muscle that catches things before they build into a spiral.

After strong feelings — sensation first, story second

When journaling, write the physical sensation before the narrative. "Chest tight, stomach dropped" before "I was upset about X." Retrains which one you notice first.

Small and consistent beats occasional and intense. The goal isn't to feel everything today — it's to lower the threshold, gradually, so things register while they're still small.

When stuck, compare instead

"Like a fist." "Like static." "Like a balloon deflating." "Like something sitting on my chest."

Metaphor is a legitimate bridge before vocabulary catches up — not cheating, just how emotional language actually develops.

Feeling it isn't the danger. It's information.
These tools just give it somewhere to go, so it never has to go through someone else.
